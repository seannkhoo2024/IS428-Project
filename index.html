01 · The problem

It's hard to tell whether a degree is worth it

Singapore's Graduate Employment Survey comes out every year as long tables, one per university, in nominal dollars. There's no single place to compare the same course across universities, see how fees differ between universities and student groups, look at more than one cohort at a time, or tell how much of a pay rise is just inflation. Fees alone can stretch the payback period for the same degree from 7.4 to 32.5 months, depending only on your fee group. And before any of that sits a simpler question nobody can easily answer.

Which degree, at which university, actually pays off? And is it worth more than starting work straight away, once you count fees, the income you give up while studying, inflation, and who is paying?

02 · Who needs this answer

Four groups, four different stakes

  • LS

    Prospective local students

    Weighing citizen-rate tuition and years out of work against starting a job straight away, and against the same degree at another university.

  • IS

    International students

    Facing much bigger fee gaps between universities, so lost income and payback time matter even more.

  • PA

    Parents

    Weighing years of tuition and the fee gap between universities against starting pay, job security and the income given up while studying.

  • PM

    Policymakers

    Checking whether a degree still pays off compared with working straight away, and where the gaps in fees and outcomes between universities are widest.

03 · Why this matters

The data to answer this is scattered

  • The stakes are personal and large

    Choosing a degree is one of the biggest financial decisions a family makes. It means years of tuition and lost income, not just a matter of interest.

  • University averages hide programmes

    A university's median depends on which programmes it offers. The same course can pay very differently from one campus to the next.

  • One year is not a trend

    Results change with every cohort and with inflation. One strong year can look like a lasting advantage when it isn't.

  • Fees aren't one number

    Citizen, PR, ASEAN, other international and non-subsidised fees can differ by 4–5 times for the same programme, so whether a degree is "worth it" depends on who's paying.

  • Pay and security are different questions

    A high median salary doesn't tell you how many graduates actually get a full-time permanent job, and the published tables rarely show both together.

  • Nominal numbers overstate progress

    Pay is usually reported in the dollars of the year it was earned. Without adjusting for inflation, ten years of pay rises look bigger than they really are.

What we add to existing GES tools: 13 graduating cohorts (2013–2025) in one place, all in 2024 dollars; the middle range of salaries (25th to 75th percentile), not just the median; a reliability flag on every figure; and tuition fees for five student groups.

Chapter 1 · The puzzle

Graduates earn more, but fewer land permanent jobs

Real median starting pay rose from S$3,577 in 2013 to S$4,430 in 2025. Over the same period, the share in full-time permanent jobs fell to 73.4% in 2025, the lowest since the COVID year.

Employment mix (right chart)

  • Full-time permanent
  • Employed, not full-time permanent
  • Overall employment rate (line)

How to read each chart

  1. KPI tiles (top): two headline figures for 2025, median starting pay and the full-time permanent rate. They set the scene; there's nothing to click.
  2. Real pay trend (line): year runs along the bottom (2013–2025) and real median starting pay, in 2024 S$, up the side. Hover a point for that year's figure.
  3. Employment mix (stacked area + line): year along the bottom, share of graduates (0–100%) up the side. Green is full-time permanent jobs and purple is other work; together they make up everyone employed. The pink line marks the overall employment rate, and the space above it is graduates without a job.
What this shows
Shows real median starting pay across all six universities from 2013 to 2025, and how graduates split between full-time permanent jobs and other work, with two headline tiles for 2025.
Key insight
Real median starting pay rose 23.9% from 2013 to 2025, with a dip in 2021 (S$4,034). The full-time permanent rate fell to 69.0% in 2020, recovered to 88.6% in 2022, then dropped to 73.4% in 2025. Overall employment also fell, from 94.7% in 2020 to 83.3% in 2025: in 2020 most graduates found some kind of job, but in 2025 fewer found any job within six months.
Why it matters
Higher starting pay doesn't mean better job security. The drop reached every university: from 2022 to 2025, each one's median permanent rate fell (NUS 91.4% to 79.1%, NTU 87.1% to 71.4%, SUSS 83.3% to 69.7%). The next chapters look at who is affected.

  • PA
    Parents

    Remember that "pays more now" and "finds a stable job" are different things.

  • PM
    Policymakers

    Read the drop across every university as a job-market problem rather than a degree-choice one.

Information & Digital Technologies has topped the field ranking every year, while Health Sciences slid from 2nd to 8th. Inside the top fields, though, programmes differ by more than S$2,000 a month (IDT: S$4,464–6,842; Business: S$3,446–5,907).

How to read each chart

  1. Field ranking (bump chart, top): year along the bottom, rank up the side. Rank 1 is the highest median pay that year; the axis shows rank, not pay. Follow a coloured line to see a field rise or fall. Where lines cross, two fields swapped places.
  2. Programme strip plot (bottom): one row per field. Each dot is a 2025 programme, placed left to right by its median pay and coloured by university. The dashed line is the field median: dots to its right beat it, dots to its left trail it.
What this shows
Shows how each field ranks on median starting pay every year (top), counting only fields with three or more programmes that year, and where each 2025 programme sits within its field (bottom), with a dashed line at the field median.
Key insight
Information & Digital Technologies ranked 1st every year from 2013 to 2025 (2025 field median S$4,996), and Engineering was 2nd in 11 of 13 years. Health Sciences slid from 2nd (2014 to 2016) to 8th in 2025, and Arts, Design & Media stayed near the bottom (S$3,741).
Why it matters
Field choice matters, but programmes within the same field can pay very differently. Field medians only run from S$3,741 to S$4,996, yet programmes inside a single field spread wider than that. Picking a field narrows your options; the specific programme still matters, as Altonji, Blom & Meghir (2012) found for college majors.

  2. Premium vs field median (right, bars): one bar per programme in the field and year you pick, sorted by size. Blue bars point right (above the field median) and orange bars point left (below it). The longer the bar, the bigger the gap.

Key insight
Programme pay varies widely within the same discipline. Computer Science runs from S$4,489 (SIT, Computer Science in Interactive Media & Game Development) to S$6,301 (NUS), a 40% gap. Business ranges from S$4,430 to S$5,907 (33%) and Computer Engineering from S$4,464 to S$5,513 (24%). The best-paid 2025 programme is NTU's Business & Computing double degree at S$6,842.
Why it matters
Compare specific programmes, not just university rankings. University medians (SUTD S$4,868, NUS S$4,568, SMU S$4,480, NTU S$4,430, SIT S$4,184, SUSS S$3,938) depend on each university's mix of programmes. SIT's game-design Computer Science pays the least of any Computer Science programme (S$4,489), while its simulation Computer Science (S$4,922) matches SUTD.

What this shows
Shows every 2025 programme plotted by pay against permanent-job rate (left) and against pay spread (right), with median lines splitting each chart into four quadrants. On the left, bubble size is the number of graduates.
Key insight
The 118 programmes in 2025 split almost evenly across the four quadrants: 32 high pay and high employment, 30 high pay with lower employment, 27 lower pay with high employment and 29 lower on both. Pay and permanent rate are only weakly correlated (r = 0.29).
Why it matters
A higher median salary doesn't guarantee stable employment or consistent earnings. Better-paid programmes tend to have a wider spread of pay (r = 0.48): at NUS Business Administration, the middle half of salaries spans 83% of the median. The lowest permanent rates are NTU Art, Design & Media (34.8%) and NUS Landscape Architecture (40.0%).

  1. Tuition by group (left, bars): one bar per student group, showing median tuition for the whole degree. Compare bar lengths to see the gap between, say, a citizen and a non-subsidised student.
  2. Fee ladder (right, dots): each row is a programme in the chosen field, and its five dots are the five student groups, placed by the fee each pays. Dots spread far apart mean that programme's price depends a lot on who's paying.
What this shows
Shows the total tuition for a whole degree by student group (left), and the five prices each programme in the chosen field charges (right).
Key insight
Fees are for a whole degree (AY2026/27, in 2024 dollars). Median total tuition is S$32,129 for citizens, S$44,903 for PRs, S$69,871 for ASEAN students, S$82,838 for other international students and S$150,885 for non-subsidised students. Why it matters
The financial value of a degree depends heavily on your fee group. For international and non-subsidised students, cost weighs as much in the decision as the choice of programme.

How to read each chart

  1. Payback by programme (left, bars): one bar per programme in the field and student group you've picked, sorted by months to pay back tuition. Shorter bars mean better value for that group.

What this shows
Shows how many months of median starting pay it takes to cover tuition for each programme (left), and pay against payback (right).
Key insight
Median payback is 7.4 months for citizens, 10.7 for PRs, 16.4 for ASEAN students, 19.0 for other international students and 32.5 for non-subsidised students. Equal pay doesn't mean equal payback: SMU Computer Science and NUS Information Systems both pay S$5,907, but a citizen pays back in 7.6 months at SMU and 5.4 at NUS, because their fees differ.
Why it matters
Your fee group changes the payback far more than your choice of programme. Payback puts fees and pay into one number that each student can work out for their own fee group.

How to read each chart

  1. Beats field median (left, bars): one bar per programme in the chosen field. Its length is the share of years in the From–To window that it paid above the field median, labelled "X of N years". Longer bars mark steady leaders rather than one good year.
  2. Slope chart (top right): two columns, the From year and the To year; each line is a programme, sloping up or down to show whether its real pay rose or fell. Lines that cross swapped places.
  3. Supply vs pay (bottom right, scatter): x-axis is the change in number of graduates, y-axis is the change in real pay, over the same window. The points scatter with no clear slope, so a growing intake hasn't visibly pushed pay down.
What this shows
Shows how often each programme beat its field median over the chosen years (left), how its real pay moved (top right), and whether bigger intakes lowered pay (bottom right).
Key insight
From 2015 to 2025, seven programmes beat their field median in all 11 years: NUS Computer Science, NUS Business Administration (Hons), NUS Chemical Engineering, NUS Industrial & Systems Engineering, NTU Accountancy & Business, NTU Mathematical Sciences and SMU Business Management. SUTD Engineering Systems & Design beat it in all 10 years it was reported. Across 53 programmes, changes in graduate numbers weren't meaningfully related to changes in real pay (r = −0.11).
Why it matters
A run of strong years says more than one good year, and bigger intakes haven't pulled pay down, at least so far. Dentistry and other single-programme fields always equal their own median, so "beats field" doesn't apply to them.

How each stakeholder can use this

  • IS
    International students

    Favour programmes that beat their field most years, since that premium is steadier.

  • PA
    Parents

    Note that bigger intakes haven't visibly lowered pay, which eases worries about expansion.

  • PM
    Policymakers

    Check the evidence before capping a programme's intake over fears of lower pay.

How to use: choose a field and a From year / To year window. All three charts update. The left chart counts how many of the reported years in that window each programme beat its field median (e.g. "4 of 5 years" for 2015–2019). Key takeaways

Eight things we'd want a student and their parents to know, in the order the story tells them.

  1. Pay is up, but permanent jobs are down. Real starting pay rose 23.9% since 2013, but only 73.4% of 2025 graduates got full-time permanent jobs, the lowest since 2020. Just 83.3% found any job within six months, down from 94.7% in 2020.

    It hit every university: each one's median permanent rate fell from 2022 to 2025 (NUS 91.4% to 79.1%, NTU 87.1% to 71.4%, SUSS 83.3% to 69.7%).

  2. Field matters, but the programme matters more. IDT has topped the ranking every year while Health Sciences slid from 2nd to 8th, yet field medians only run from S$3,741 to S$4,996.

    Inside the top fields, programmes differ by more than S$2,000 a month (IDT: S$4,464–6,842; Business: S$3,446–5,907).

  3. The same course can pay 24–40% more at another university. Computer Science has the widest gap, at 40%.

    University averages hide this: SIT's game-design Computer Science pays S$4,489, while its simulation Computer Science pays S$4,922, level with SUTD.

  4. Pay and job security barely move together, and higher pay comes with less predictable pay. Pay and the permanent rate correlate at only r = 0.29; pay and pay spread at r = 0.48.

    Of 2025's 118 programmes, 32 did well on both pay and employment and 29 did poorly on both. A high median doesn't promise a safe or predictable outcome.

  5. Your fee group changes payback about fourfold, more than programme choice does for a citizen: 7.4 to 32.5 months.

    Equal pay can still mean different payback: SMU Computer Science and NUS Information Systems both pay S$5,907, but a citizen pays back in 7.6 vs 5.4 months. Programme and fee group add up rather than cancel out.

  6. A few programmes beat their field every year. Doing well year after year says more than one lucky year.

    NUS Computer Science, NUS Business Administration (Hons) and NTU Accountancy & Business beat their field median in every year on record (2013–2025).

  7. Bigger intakes haven't pushed pay down, even as the gap between programmes widened.

    The middle half of programme pay widened from S$233 to S$664 between 2013 and 2025, yet intake growth shows no clear link with pay changes.

  8. About 15% of figures need a second look. 240 of 1,615 programme-year rows are small samples, not reported, or have salaries withheld.

    The share of rows rated reliable varies by university (SUSS 85%, NTU and SIT 84%, SMU 82%, NUS 75%, SUTD 74%). Gaps are mostly in niche programmes and in years when a programme was new or regrouped, so check the flag before treating any one number as fact.

Recommendations

What we'd tell each group, and the finding behind each piece of advice.

LS

Local students

  • Compare the same course across universities, not university averages. Computer Science alone ranges from S$4,489 to S$6,301 in 2025, a 40% gap that a university's overall median hides. Business varies by 33% and Computer Engineering by 24%.
  • Check job security as well as pay. Real pay is up 23.9% since 2013, but only 73.4% of 2025 graduates got full-time permanent jobs, the lowest since 2020. Every university fell from 2022 to 2025 (NUS 91.4% to 79.1%, NTU 87.1% to 71.4%, SUSS 83.3% to 69.7%).
  • Treat the citizen payback as the best case. At about 7 months, it's easy to assume any degree pays for itself quickly. For other fee groups, the same degree takes up to four times as long.
  • Check the reliability flag before trusting any single programme. About 15% of programme-year rows are small samples, not reported, or have salaries withheld. Without the flag, a 100% permanent rate from 3 respondents looks the same as one from 300.
IS

International students

  • Work out payback for your own fee group, not the citizen default. The same degree takes about 7 months to pay back for a citizen and about 33 for a non-subsidised student. That fourfold gap matters more to you than field choice does to a citizen.
  • Weigh a falling permanent-job rate against fees you can't easily recover. If a programme's permanent rate is slipping, the risk hits harder when you've paid several times the citizen fee.
  • Pick programmes that do well on both pay and job security. Only 32 of 118 programmes did in 2025. Pay and the permanent rate correlate at just r = 0.29, so a high salary alone says little about job security.
  • Look for consistency across cohorts, not one good year. A programme that beats its field most years is a safer bet than one riding a single strong cohort, since you commit years and a large fee before you know which kind you picked.
PA

Parents

  • Treat "pays more" and "finds a stable job" as two separate questions. Real pay rose 23.9% since 2013 while the permanent rate fell to its lowest since 2020. A programme can look better on one and worse on the other.
  • Count the whole cost, not just tuition. The fee gap between student groups adds to the income given up while studying. A cheaper university can still mean a longer payback if its pay doesn't keep up.
  • Look past the median. Better-paid programmes tend to have a wider spread of outcomes (r = 0.48). At NUS Business Administration, the middle half of salaries spans 83% of the median, so the "typical" graduate may not look like your child.
  • Don't let one flagship programme set expectations for its whole field. University averages can hide programmes that are far weaker or stronger than the headline suggests.
PM

Policymakers

  • Treat the drop in permanent jobs as a job-market problem: it happened at every university. Every university's median permanent rate fell between 2022 and 2025, so capping specific programme intakes over fears of lower pay would target the wrong lever.
  • Target support using both pay and employment. 29 of 118 programmes in 2025 were low on both, a more urgent group than the 30 that pay well but offer less security.
  • Check that subsidies keep up with the payback gap between fee groups. Fee group alone turns a payback of about 7 months into about 33. It's worth asking whether Tuition Grant and subsidy levels keep pace, especially for PR and ASEAN students.
  • Quote programme-level numbers carefully. About 15% of rows are small samples, not reported, or have salaries withheld, so check the flag before building a policy argument on one programme's figure.
GES

Universities and MOE

  • Publish respondent numbers for every programme, as the 2025 survey table started doing. Without them, a 100% permanent rate from 3 respondents looks the same as one from 300.
  • Give each programme an ID that stays the same when it's renamed. Renamed or merged programmes break any attempt to follow one programme over time.
  • Publish the 10th and 90th percentiles too. The middle half of salaries already shows wide spreads; the tails would show how far outcomes really stretch.
  • Publish fees next to graduate outcomes so families can work out payback. Pay tables and fee schedules now sit on separate pages, which makes the payback question harder than it needs to be.

Data sources

We combined four public datasets from 2013 to 2025 into one table of 1,615 programme-years covering 183 programmes. The Graduate Employment Survey comes in two parts: 2013–2024 from data.gov.sg and 2025 from MOM's Table T15.

Surveys (outcomes)

  • Graduate Employment Survey, 2013–2024, for NTU, NUS, SIT, SMU, SUSS and SUTD (MOE, via data.gov.sg): 1,550 rows of employment rates and salary percentiles by degree.
  • GES 2025, Table T15 (MOM): 148 degree rows, which also give the response rate and "secured employment".

Counts and costs

  • Graduates from Institutions of Higher Learning, 2015–2025 (MOM): graduate numbers by course.
  • AY2026/27 tuition fee schedules, from each university's own fee pages. We matched 140 of 150 current programmes.

Inflation adjustment

  • Consumer Price Index, monthly, 2024 = 100 (SingStat): used to turn salaries and fees into 2024 dollars.
Cleaning and the course crosswalk

A scripted pipeline turns 458 raw degree titles from six universities into 183 programmes you can follow year to year. Every merge follows a written rule that can be rerun.

What the pipeline fixes

One command rebuilds every table from the seven source files and leaves logs and QA files behind as an audit trail. It fixes garbled school names, moves the footnote symbols on degree titles into flags (small sample, not reported, follow-up survey, merged programme), and turns 928 "na" cells into blanks.

How the crosswalk works

Each of the 458 raw titles maps to one programme, discipline and field (using MOE's GES 2025 course groupings) under a written rule. A degree that was simply renamed becomes one continuous series. Programmes that ran side by side under similar names stay separate, such as NUS Honours and non-Honours, or SIT Chemical Engineering with Newcastle and with TU Munich.

What we checked

Every programme has exactly one row per year and exactly one field and discipline. All 1,480 cells in the 2025 rows match Table T15, with no mismatches.

Real dollars: how we adjust for inflation

Every salary and fee is restated in 2024 dollars, so figures from different years can be compared directly.

real = nominal × CPI (2024 average) ÷ CPI (survey month), using the all-items CPI for the month each university actually ran its survey, rather than a yearly average:

  • November for NUS, NTU, SMU and SUSS
  • March of the following year for SIT
  • February of the following year for SUTD, until it moved to November in 2024
  • November 2025 for all six in the 2025 round

Using the survey month instead of the yearly average changes values by 0.39% at the median and by up to 4.29%. Tuition is adjusted separately, using the August 2026 CPI, when AY2026/27 starts.

Assumptions

Read every chart as early-career outcomes, in real dollars, compared within similar fields. It isn't a lifetime ranking, and it doesn't show cause and effect.

  1. Employment prospects mean GES results about six months after graduation, mainly the full-time permanent employment rate and gross monthly salary.
  2. All money is in 2024 dollars, so amounts from different years compare directly.
  3. Outcomes are ranges, not single numbers: each programme-year shows its median plus the 25th and 75th percentiles.
  4. Universities are compared within a field or discipline, never across unrelated ones.
  5. Small samples and late surveys are set aside, not hidden. Rows under 30 respondents stay out of headline views. Law, Medicine, Pharmacy, Architecture and BMS & Chinese Medicine graduates are surveyed later, so they're left out of fresh-graduate charts.
  6. Student groups follow each university's own fee categories: Singapore Citizen, Permanent Resident, International (ASEAN), International (Other) and Non-subsidised (no Tuition Grant).
Payback definition

Payback months = total tuition (real) ÷ median gross monthly starting pay (real). It's how many months of a median starting salary it takes to cover the whole tuition bill.

Total tuition is each university's annual fee × programme length. SUSS publishes a programme total, and SIT charges per credit, so for SIT it's fee per credit × total credits. We use 2025 salaries only, because the fees are for AY2026/27, so payback doesn't allow for pay growth in between.

It also leaves out tax, CPF, living costs, bursaries and other financial aid, and loans. Treat it as a rough affordability ratio, not a return on investment.

Limitations

Treat this as a snapshot of early-career outcomes. It isn't a cause-and-effect ranking or a full cost-benefit analysis.

What GES does and doesn't capture

  • It measures outcomes about six months after graduation, so it shows how graduates enter the job market, not how their careers go.
  • Response rates are below 100%, and we know nothing about people who didn't respond.
  • GES publishes only three points for each salary range: the 25th percentile, median and 75th percentile.

Comparisons and counts

  • Comparisons describe what happened; they don't show cause, because programmes admit different students (Altonji, Blom & Meghir, 2012).
  • MOM counts graduates, not survey respondents. 2025 respondent numbers are estimates, and most NUS and SUSS counts can't be linked to survey rows.
  • A few documented crosswalk judgement calls can shift individual programme series.

Payback and scope

  • Payback leaves out tax, CPF, living costs, financial aid, loans and pay growth, and pairs AY2026/27 fees with 2025 salaries.
  • Polytechnic GES isn't included yet.
