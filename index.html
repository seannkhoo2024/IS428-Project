01 · The problem

We don't know if a degree is even worth it

Singapore's Graduate Employment Survey is published every year as long per-university tables in nominal dollars. There is no single place to compare the same discipline across universities, see the fee gap between universities and student groups, see more than one cohort at a time, or know how much of any pay gain is just inflation — the fee gap alone changes the payback period for the same degree from 7.4 to 32.5 months, depending only on which fee group you're in. And before any of that, there's a more basic question nobody can easily answer.

Which degree, at which university, actually pays off — and is it worth more than starting work right away, once you account for fees, years of foregone income, inflation, and who is paying?

02 · Who needs this answer

Four groups, four different stakes

  • LS

    Prospective local students

    Weighing citizen-rate tuition and years out of the workforce against simply starting work, and against the same degree at another university.

  • IS

    International students

    Facing a much wider fee gap across universities, where foregone income and payback period matter even more.

  • PA

    Parents

    Weighing the fee gap between universities and years of tuition against starting pay, job security, and the income given up while studying.

  • PM

    Policymakers

    Checking whether a degree still pays off over working straight away, and where fee and outcome gaps between universities are widest.

03 · Why this matters

The data to answer this is scattered

  • The stakes are personal and large

    Choosing a degree is one of the biggest financial decisions a family makes — years of tuition and foregone income, not just curiosity.

  • University averages hide programmes

    A university's median depends on its mix of programmes. The same discipline can pay very differently from one campus to the next.

  • One year is not a trend

    Outcomes move with each cohort and with inflation. A strong year can look like a lasting advantage when it isn't.

  • Fees aren't one number

    Citizen, PR, ASEAN, other international and non-subsidised rates can differ by 4–5x for the same programme, so "is it worth it" has a different answer for each stakeholder.

  • Pay and security are different questions

    A high median salary says nothing about how many graduates actually land a full-time permanent job. Published tables rarely let you see both at once.

  • Nominal numbers overstate progress

    Pay figures are usually reported in the dollars of the day they were earned. Without adjusting for inflation, a decade of raises can look bigger than it really is.

We go further than existing GES tools: thirteen cohorts (2013–2025) in real 2024 dollars, the P25–P75 range and not only the median, reliability flags on every number, and tuition fees for five student groups.

Chapter 1 · The puzzle

Graduates earn more, but fewer land permanent jobs

Real median starting pay rose from S$3,577 to S$4,430 between 2013 and 2025. Yet the share in full-time permanent jobs fell to 73.4% in 2025, the lowest since the COVID year.

  2. Real pay trend (line): x-axis is year (2013–2025), y-axis is real median starting pay in 2024 S$. Read it left to right as one continuous trend; hover a point for that year's exact figure.
  3. Employment mix (stacked area + line): x-axis is year again, y-axis is share of graduates (0–100%). What this shows
Two tiles give the 2025 median pay and full-time permanent rate. The line traces real median starting pay from 2013 to 2025. The stacked area splits employment into full-time permanent and other jobs, with the overall employment rate as a line.
Key insight
Pay rose 23.9% in real terms, with a dip in 2021 (S$4,034). The permanent rate bottomed at 69.0% in 2020, recovered to 88.6% in 2022, then fell to 73.4%. Overall employment was 94.7% in 2020 but only 83.3% in 2025: in 2020 most graduates found some job, in 2025 fewer found any job within six months.
Why it matters
The "average graduate" story hides a job-security squeeze, and it is broad-based: every university's median permanent rate fell from 2022 to 2025 (NUS 91.4% to 79.1%, NTU 87.1% to 71.4%, SUSS 83.3% to 69.7%). The rest of the site looks at who is affected.

Key insight

Key insight
From 2015 to 2025, eight programmes beat their field median in all 11 years: NUS Computer Science, NUS Business Administration (Hons), NUS Chemical Engineering, NUS Industrial & Systems Engineering, NUS Nursing (Hons), NTU Accountancy & Business, NTU Mathematical Sciences and SMU Business Management. Key takeaways

Six things we'd want a student and their parents to know, in the order the story tells them.

  1. Real starting pay is up about a quarter since 2013, but full-time permanent employment is at its lowest since 2020 (73.4%).

    The slide is broad-based, not a weak-sector problem: every university's median permanent rate fell between 2022 and 2025.

  2. Field matters (IDT leads every year; Health Sciences slid from 2nd to 8th), but the spread within a field is larger than the gaps between most fields.

    Field medians run S$3,741–4,996, a narrower range than the spread of programmes inside most single fields.

  3. The same discipline can differ by 25–40% across universities (Computer Science: 40%).

    University-level medians hide this: a university's game-design Computer Science can pay like an arts degree while its other Computer Science programme matches the top schools.

  4. Pay and job security are only weakly related (r = 0.29), and higher pay comes with a wider spread.

    2025's 118 programmes split almost evenly across high/low pay and high/low employment, so no single profile dominates.

  5. Fees by student group change payback roughly fourfold (7.4 to 32.5 months), more than field choice does for citizens.

    Two programmes with identical pay can have very different paybacks once fees are added: SMU Computer Science and NUS Information Systems both pay S$5,907, but citizen payback is 7.6 vs 5.4 months.

  6. The gap between programmes widened from S$233 to S$664 (interquartile range, 2013 → 2025), a small set of programmes beat their field every year, and graduate supply has not visibly depressed pay.

    NUS Computer Science, NUS Business Administration (Hons) and NTU Accountancy & Business beat their field median in all 13 years on record.

Recommendations

What we'd tell each group, and the finding behind each piece of advice.

ST

Prospective students and parents

  • Compare within a discipline, not by university average. Computer Science alone spans S$4,489–6,301 across universities, a 40% range a school's overall median would hide.
  • Check full-time permanent rates and the P25–P75 range, not only the median. A high median pay says nothing about how many graduates actually land a permanent job, or how wide the outcomes are around it.
  • Use payback for your own student group. Citizen, PR, ASEAN, other international and non-subsidised rates can turn a 7-month payback into one over four times longer.
  • Treat programmes flagged small-sample or not reported with caution. About 15% of all programme-year rows in this dataset rest on fewer than 30 respondents or have no reported figure at all.
GES

Universities and MOE

  • Publish GES with response counts for every row, as Table T15 began doing in 2025. Without it, a 100% permanent rate from 3 respondents looks identical to one from 300.
  • Keep a consistent programme identifier across years. Renamed or merged programmes break any attempt to track a single programme's trend over time.
  • Publish P10 and P90 if possible. The P25–P75 range already used here shows wide spreads; the tails would show just how wide outcomes really get.
  • Report fees alongside outcomes. Pay tables and fee schedules currently live on separate pages, making the payback question harder to answer than it needs to be.
V2

Future versions of this tool

  • Add polytechnic GES for diploma-versus-degree context. Right now this tool can't answer whether a degree beats a diploma in the same field.
  • Add longer-run outcomes when they become available. A six-month employment snapshot says little about a 10- or 20-year career.
  • Offer national wage benchmarks as optional reference lines. So a programme's pay can be read against the wider labour market, not only against other programmes.

Data sources

Six public datasets, 2013–2025, merged into one table of 1,615 programme-years across 183 programmes.

Surveys (outcomes)

  • Graduate Employment Survey, 2013–2024 — NTU, NUS, SIT, SMU, SUSS, SUTD (MOE, via data.gov.sg). 1,550 rows: employment rate and salary percentiles per degree.
  • GES 2025, Table T15 (MOM). 148 degree rows, with response rate and "secured employment" added.

Counts and costs

  • Graduates from Institutions of Higher Learning, 2015–2025 (MOM) — graduate counts per course.
  • AY2026/27 tuition fee schedules — each university's own fee pages. 140 of 150 current programmes mapped.

Benchmarks

  • Consumer Price Index, 2024 = 100, monthly (SingStat) — used to deflate salaries and fees to real dollars.
  • Labour Force Survey income tables (MOM/SingStat) — national wage context only, never used for ranking.
Cleaning and the course crosswalk

A scripted pipeline turns 458 raw degree titles from six universities into 183 programmes you can compare year over year — every merge is a documented, repeatable rule.

What the pipeline fixes

One command rebuilds every table from the seven source files, with logs and QA files left behind as an audit trail. It repairs garbled school names, splits footnote symbols off degree titles into flags (small sample, not reported, follow-up survey, merged programme), and turns 928 "na" cells into blanks.

How the crosswalk works

Each of the 458 raw titles maps to one canonical programme, discipline and field (the MOE GES 2025 clusters) under an explicit, documented rule. Pure renamings become one continuous series. Programmes that ran side by side under similar names stay separate — e.g. NUS Honours vs. non-Honours, or SIT Chemical Engineering with Newcastle vs. with TU Munich.

What we checked

Exactly one row per programme per year, exactly one field and discipline per programme, and all 1,480 cells of the 2025 rows matched against Table T15 with zero mismatches.

Real dollars: how we adjust for inflation

Every salary and fee is restated in 2024 dollars, so figures from different survey years are comparable.

real = nominal × CPI(2024 average) ÷ CPI(survey month), using CPI All Items for the month each university actually surveyed — not a flat annual average.

  • November, for NUS, NTU, SMU and SUSS
  • March of the following year, for SIT
  • February of the following year for SUTD, until it moved to November from 2024
  • November 2025, for all six, in the 2025 round

Using the survey month instead of the annual average changes values by a median of 0.39% and up to 4.29%. Tuition is deflated separately, with August 2026 CPI — the start of AY2026/27.

Read every chart as: early-career outcomes, in real dollars, compared within similar fields — not a lifetime or causal ranking.

  1. Employment prospects are GES outcomes about six months after graduation — mainly the full-time permanent employment rate and gross monthly salary.
  2. All money is in real 2024 dollars, so amounts from different years can be compared directly.
  3. Outcomes are distributions, not single numbers: each programme-year is shown with its median, P25 and P75.
  4. Comparisons across universities are made within a field or discipline, not across unrelated ones.
  5. Small samples and late surveys are set aside, not hidden: rows under 30 respondents are left out of headline views; Law, Medicine, Pharmacy, Architecture and BMS & Chinese Medicine are surveyed later and excluded from fresh-graduate charts.
  6. National occupational wages are field-level context only, never used to rank programmes.
  7. Student groups follow each university's own fee categories: Singapore Citizen, Permanent Resident, International (ASEAN), International (Other), and Non-subsidised (no Tuition Grant).
Payback definition

Payback months = total tuition (real) ÷ median gross monthly starting pay (real). How many months of a median starting salary it takes to cover the whole tuition bill.

Fees are each university's annual fee × programme length (SUSS: programme total; SIT: fee per credit × total credits). Payback uses 2025 salaries only, because the fees are AY2026/27 fees — it does not model salary growth between now and then.

It also ignores taxes, CPF, living costs, bursaries and other financial aid, and loans. Treat it as a rough affordability ratio, not a return-on-investment calculation.

Limitations

Treat this as a descriptive snapshot of early-career outcomes — not a causal ranking, and not a full cost-benefit analysis.

What GES does and doesn't capture

  • It measures outcomes about six months after graduation — labour-market entry, not careers.
  • Response rates are below 100%, and non-respondents are not profiled.
  • GES publishes only three points per distribution: P25, median and P75.

Comparisons and counts

  • Comparisons are descriptive, not causal — programmes differ in who they admit (Altonji, Blom & Meghir, 2012).
  • MOM's graduate counts are graduates, not survey respondents; 2025 respondent counts are estimates; NUS and SUSS counts are mostly unlinkable to the survey rows.
  • A handful of documented crosswalk judgement calls can shift individual series.

Payback and scope

  • Payback ignores taxes, CPF, living costs, financial aid, loans and salary growth, and pairs AY2026/27 fees with 2025 salaries.
  • Wage benchmarks are context only. Polytechnic GES is not included yet.
